# Japanese Basics

## How Japanese Is Written

Japanese uses three writing systems simultaneously, and most sentences mix all three. Understanding *why* each script exists makes the whole system feel less overwhelming.

### The Three Scripts

**Hiragana** (ひらがな) is the backbone of the written language — 46 base characters, each representing one syllable (*ka*, *mi*, *te*, etc.). It covers native Japanese words, grammatical particles, verb endings, and anything with no kanji equivalent. If you know no kanji, you can write everything in hiragana and be understood.

**Katakana** (カタカナ) represents the exact same sounds as hiragana but is visually distinct — angular where hiragana is rounded. It marks foreign loanwords, borrowed proper nouns, scientific terms, onomatopoeia, and emphasis. Seeing katakana is a reliable signal: this word came from somewhere else.

**Kanji** (漢字) are Chinese-origin characters that carry meaning directly. A single kanji can pack the meaning of a whole English word into one character. The 常用漢字 standard defines 2,136 characters used in everyday writing; fluent readers know several thousand.

A real sentence often uses all three at once:

| Part | Script | Role |
|---|---|---|
| 私 | Kanji | "I / me" |
| は | Hiragana | topic particle |
| コーヒー | Katakana | "coffee" (loanword) |
| が | Hiragana | subject particle |
| 好きです。| Kanji + Hiragana | "like" + polite ending |

> **私はコーヒーが好きです。**  
> *Watashi wa koohii ga suki desu.*  
> I like coffee.

> **Note:** Reading a Japanese sentence is not harder once you accept that the script-switching is carrying information. Katakana = likely foreign word. Kanji = meaning anchor. Hiragana = grammatical glue.

## Sentence Structure

### SOV Word Order

Japanese is a **Subject–Object–Verb** language — the verb always comes last. This is the single biggest structural difference from English, and it affects everything: you have to hold the subject and object in your head before the action resolves at the end of the sentence.

| English (SVO) | Japanese (SOV) | Romaji |
|---|---|---|
| I **drink** coffee. | 私はコーヒーを**飲みます**。| Watashi wa koohii wo **nomimasu**. |
| She **reads** books. | 彼女は本を**読みます**。| Kanojo wa hon wo **yomimasu**. |
| He **bought** a bag. | 彼はかばんを**買いました**。| Kare wa kaban wo **kaimashita**. |
| The dog **is chasing** the cat. | 犬が猫を**追いかけています**。| Inu ga neko wo **oikakete imasu**. |

Modifiers also stack *before* what they modify, not after. Adjectives, clauses, and relative phrases all come first — then the noun they describe.

> **Note:** In long sentences, native speakers can reach the verb at the very end and finally know what happened. This is why listening in Japanese feels like "waiting for the punchline." With practice, you start anticipating the verb class early from context.

---

### Topic は vs Subject が

Japanese draws a distinction that English collapses into one slot. **は** marks the *topic* — what the sentence is *about*. **が** marks the *subject* — who or what *performs* or *experiences* the predicate.

```
[Topic] は [Subject] が [Predicate]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English | What's happening |
|---|---|---|---|
| 猫は魚が好きです。| Neko wa sakana ga suki desu. | Cats like fish. | 猫は = topic; 魚が = subject of 好き |
| 私は頭が痛いです。| Watashi wa atama ga itai desu. | My head hurts. | 私は = topic; 頭が = subject of 痛い |
| 象は鼻が長い。| Zō wa hana ga nagai. | Elephants have long trunks. | 象は = topic; 鼻が = subject of 長い |
| この映画は私が好きです。| Kono eiga wa watashi ga suki desu. | As for this movie, I like it. | 映画は = topic; 私が = the one who likes it |

> **Note:** は often translates as "as for ~" or "speaking of ~" — it sets the frame for everything that follows. が identifies *who* is doing or experiencing something. Many beginners use が everywhere at first; は is how you signal that you're talking *about* something, not just naming it.

> **Common mistake:** Using が when は is correct makes sentences sound like new information or contrast is being introduced. 私**が**田中です would mean "It is *I* who is Tanaka" — implying there was a question about which person. 私**は**田中です is just "I am Tanaka."

### Modifier Placement

In English, modifiers can appear on either side of a noun ("the tall man" or "the man who is tall"). In Japanese, they always come *before* the noun they describe — adjectives, relative clauses, and entire subordinate phrases all stack up before their noun.

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 大きい犬 | ōkii inu | big dog |
| 赤い車 | akai kuruma | red car |
| 私が買った本 | watashi ga katta hon | the book I bought |
| 昨日見た映画 | kinō mita eiga | the movie I watched yesterday |
| 田中さんが作ったケーキ | Tanaka-san ga tsukutta kēki | the cake that Tanaka made |

> **Note:** Relative clauses in Japanese require no relative pronoun (no "that," "which," or "who"). You simply place the entire clause before the noun: 昨日読んだ = "read-yesterday" stacks directly on any following noun.

---

### Subject Dropping

Japanese regularly omits the subject when it's clear from context. This is called *pro-drop*, and it's the rule rather than the exception in natural speech. Writing or saying わかりました is not incomplete — the subject is understood.

| Japanese | Romaji | Implied subject |
|---|---|---|
| 食べます。| Tabemasu. | I / you / we eat. |
| 行きますか。| Ikimasu ka. | Are you going? |
| わかりました。| Wakarimashita. | (I) understood. |
| 来ましたよ。| Kimashita yo. | (They've) arrived. |

> **Note:** What looks like ambiguity is resolved by context. Once "I" or "she" has been established as the topic, Japanese speakers don't keep repeating it. If you're not sure who the subject is in a real conversation, that's normal — context does the work.

## Punctuation & Spacing

Japanese has no spaces between words. Word boundaries are legible because the three scripts alternate predictably, and because readers have internalised the grammar. The punctuation system is its own, parallel to the Western system.

### Punctuation Marks

| Symbol | Name | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 。| Kuten | Sentence-ending period |
| 、| Touten | Comma — marks a pause within a sentence |
| 「 」| Kagikakko | Primary quotation marks |
| 『 』| Nijū kagikakko | Nested quotation marks, or book/work titles |
| ・ | Nakaguro | Separates list items or parts of a foreign name |
| ー | Chōonpu | Extends a vowel sound in katakana words |
| ！ ？ | Full-width forms | Same function as English, but full-width for alignment |

> **Note:** In Japanese fiction and manga, 「」kagikakko are the standard dialogue markers — not "quotation marks" as in Western style. You'll encounter them constantly in native texts.

### Examples in Context

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 「おはよう」と言いました。| "Ohayō" to iimashita. | (She) said "Good morning." |
| 私はりんご、みかん、ぶどうを買いました。| Watashi wa ringo, mikan, budō wo kaimashita. | I bought apples, oranges, and grapes. |
| スミス・ジョン | Sumisu Jon | John Smith (foreign name with nakaguro) |

### Writing Direction

Modern Japanese is written horizontally, left to right. Traditional texts — novels, some newspapers, manga speech bubbles — run *vertically* in columns read right to left. Both directions are in active use today.

## Small Kana & Sound Rules

Three special small kana change pronunciation in systematic ways. They are not decorative — getting them wrong changes the word.

### っ — Double Consonant (Sokuon)

A small っ signals a short, sharp pause before the following consonant, which is then held slightly longer (doubled). It occupies one full mora — one beat of rhythm — like any other kana.

```
[kana] + っ + [kana starting with a consonant]
→ the pause doubles the following consonant
```

| Written | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| きって | kitte | postage stamp |
| ざっし | zasshi | magazine |
| いっぽ | ippo | one step |
| きっぷ | kippu | ticket |
| ちょっと | chotto | a little / just a moment |

> **Common mistake:** Without the っ, the meaning changes entirely. きて (*kite*) = "come!" — completely different from きって (*kitte*, postage stamp). Treat っ as a real beat of silence, not an ornament.

---

### ゃ ゅ ょ — Compound Sounds (Yōon)

Small や, ゆ, よ combine with a preceding consonant + i kana to form a single blended syllable. The i vowel disappears and the two characters collapse into one sound.

```
[consonant + i kana] + ゃ / ゅ / ょ → one syllable, not two
```

| Written | Romaji | One syllable? | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| きゃ | kya | yes | not *ki-ya* |
| しゅ | shu | yes | not *shi-yu* |
| にょ | nyo | yes | — |
| びょういん | byōin | hospital — 4 mora: びょ・う・い・ん | often read as 5 |

> **Note:** びょういん (hospital) and びよういん (beauty salon) are frequently confused — the first has a small よ (1 syllable), the second has a full-size よ (2 syllables). The written distinction is crucial.

---

### Long Vowels

Holding a vowel for one extra mora changes meaning. Vowel length is phonemically significant in Japanese — two otherwise identical words can differ only in how long a vowel is held.

| Short | Romaji | Long | Romaji | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| おじさん | ojisan | おじいさん | ojiisan | uncle vs grandfather |
| ここ | koko | こうこう | kōkō | here vs high school |
| おばさん | obasan | おばあさん | obaasan | aunt vs grandmother |
| ビル | biru | ビール | bīru | building vs beer |

**How long vowels are written:**

```
Hiragana: repeat the vowel (or use お + う for the ō sound)
  おかあさん = okaasan  (mother)
  こうこう   = kōkō     (high school)

Katakana: use ー (chōonpu)
  コーヒー = koohii   (coffee)
  スーパー = suupaa   (supermarket)
```

> **Note:** The ー mark only appears in katakana. In hiragana words, the long vowel is spelled out explicitly (ああ, おう, etc.), which is why romanisation can look awkward. Romaji like *kōkō* is shorthand — the actual hiragana spells こうこう.

## Pitch Accent

English stress makes one syllable *louder*. Japanese **pitch accent** makes one syllable *higher* in tone. Every word has a pitch pattern, and in some cases the pattern is the only thing distinguishing two otherwise identical words.

The Tokyo dialect (standard Japanese) is the reference system used in textbooks and broadcast media.

### High and Low Patterns

Pitch is described as H (high) or L (low) per mora.

| Word | Reading | Pitch | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| 橋 | hashi | L-H | bridge |
| 箸 | hashi | H-L | chopsticks |
| 端 | hashi | H-L | edge |
| 雨 | ame | L-H | rain |
| 飴 | ame | H-L | candy |
| 東京 | tōkyō | L-H-H-L | Tokyo |

> **Note:** 橋 and 箸 look identical in romaji and share the same kanji sounds — pitch is the *only* distinguishing feature. In a real sentence, context almost always makes meaning clear before pitch even matters.

### For Learners

Getting pitch accent wrong does not prevent communication. Native speakers adjust easily for non-native speech. Pitch is best absorbed through listening — it improves naturally with exposure and can be studied deliberately at intermediate level.

For now: focus on vocabulary, grammar, and reading. If you hear a native speaker and something sounds "off" about your pronunciation, pitch is often the reason — but it is a refinement, not a foundation.
