# Grammar — N5

## Chapter 1 — Verbs

### Verb Groups

Every Japanese verb belongs to one of three groups. The group determines *every* conjugation rule that applies to it — get the group right and the rest is predictable. Get it wrong and your conjugations will be consistently off.

**Group 1 — Godan (五段):** ends in any う-column sound — く, ぐ, す, つ, ぬ, ぶ, む, う, or る*. The stem changes shape across forms following phonetic patterns.

**Group 2 — Ichidan (一段):** ends in る, with an い or え vowel sound immediately before it. The stem never changes — you only ever remove the る. Nothing else.

**Group 3 — Irregular:** only する (to do) and くる (to come). Both must be memorised.

| Japanese | Romaji | English | Group |
|---|---|---|---|
| 書く | kaku | to write | 1 |
| 話す | hanasu | to speak | 1 |
| 待つ | matsu | to wait | 1 |
| 帰る | kaeru | to return home | 1 *(looks like Group 2)* |
| 食べる | taberu | to eat | 2 |
| 見る | miru | to see | 2 |
| 起きる | okiru | to wake up | 2 |
| する | suru | to do | 3 |
| 来る | kuru | to come | 3 |

> **Common mistake:** る-ending Group 1 verbs look identical to Group 2 in dictionary form. 帰る (*kaeru*, to return), 走る (*hashiru*, to run), 知る (*shiru*, to know), 切る (*kiru*, to cut) are all Group 1. There's no reliable visual test — when in doubt, check a dictionary. Over time you'll recognise them by exposure.

---

### Polite Present / Future — ～ます

The ます form is how you speak politely in Japanese. It's the default for conversations with people you don't know well, in formal situations, and in service environments. It expresses both present habit and future intention — Japanese doesn't separate these.

```
Group 1: change the final u-vowel to i-vowel + ます
  書く  → 書き + ます  → 書きます
  話す  → 話し + ます  → 話します
  待つ  → 待ち + ます  → 待ちます

Group 2: remove る + ます
  食べる → 食べ + ます → 食べます
  見る   → 見 + ます   → 見ます

Group 3:
  する → します
  来る → 来ます (きます)

Negative: replace ます with ません
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 毎朝コーヒーを飲みます。| Maiasa koohii wo nomimasu. | I drink coffee every morning. |
| 明日、友達に会います。| Ashita, tomodachi ni aimasu. | I'll meet a friend tomorrow. |
| 肉を食べません。| Niku wo tabemasen. | I don't eat meat. |
| 週末は映画を見ます。| Shūmatsu wa eiga wo mimasu. | I watch movies on weekends. |
| 日本語を勉強します。| Nihongo wo benkyō shimasu. | I study Japanese. |

> **Note:** ～ます covers both habits ("I drink coffee every morning") and planned future actions ("I'll meet a friend tomorrow"). Context makes the meaning clear. There is no separate future tense in Japanese.

> **Register:** ます is polite but not formal. You'd use it with a shop clerk, a new acquaintance, or a teacher. Among close friends, speakers drop to the plain (dictionary) form. Both are grammatically correct — register determines which fits.

---

### Polite Past — ～ました

The past tense is formed by replacing ます with ました. The negative past uses ませんでした. Both follow the same stem rules as the present tense.

```
Affirmative past: [stem] + ました
Negative past:    [stem] + ませんでした
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 昨日、映画を見ました。| Kinō, eiga wo mimashita. | I watched a movie yesterday. |
| 朝ごはんを食べませんでした。| Asagohan wo tabemasen deshita. | I didn't eat breakfast. |
| 先週、東京に行きました。| Senshū, Tōkyō ni ikimashita. | I went to Tokyo last week. |
| レポートを書きませんでした。| Repōto wo kakimasen deshita. | I didn't write the report. |
| 先生に電話しました。| Sensei ni denwa shimashita. | I called the teacher. |

> **Note:** Japanese past tense is clean — no distinction between simple past ("I ate") and perfect ("I have eaten") in the polite form. Context and time words carry that distinction.

## Chapter 2 — Direction and Location

Particles carry information that English relies on prepositions for. Three particles — へ, に, and で — each handle a specific spatial role.

### へ / に — Direction and Destination

Both へ and に can mark a destination when paired with a movement verb. The difference is subtle: へ emphasises the direction of travel; に emphasises arrival at the endpoint. In most sentences either works, and に is more common in modern speech.

```
[Place] へ / に + [movement verb]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 学校へ行きます。| Gakkō e ikimasu. | I go to school. |
| 東京に来ました。| Tōkyō ni kimashita. | I came to Tokyo. |
| 家に帰ります。| Uchi ni kaerimasu. | I go home. |
| 駅のほうへ歩きました。| Eki no hō e arukimashita. | I walked toward the station. |
| 日本に引っ越しました。| Nihon ni hikkoshimashita. | I moved to Japan. |

> **Note:** に is also used with arrival verbs like 着く (*tsuku*, to arrive) and 帰る (*kaeru*, to return). In these cases に marks the destination and is not interchangeable with へ. 家に帰る = arrive back home. 家へ帰る = head toward home.

---

### で — Location of an Action

で marks *where an action takes place* — the venue or instrument of an activity. It is distinct from に, which marks *where something exists*.

```
[Place] で + [action verb]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 図書館で勉強します。| Toshokan de benkyō shimasu. | I study at the library. |
| 公園で走りました。| Kōen de hashirimashita. | I ran in the park. |
| 駅前のカフェでコーヒーを飲みます。| Ekimae no kafe de koohii wo nomimasu. | I drink coffee at the café near the station. |
| 電車で本を読みます。| Densha de hon wo yomimasu. | I read books on the train. |
| 日本語で話してください。| Nihongo de hanashite kudasai. | Please speak in Japanese. |

> **Common mistake:** で vs に for location is one of the most common early errors. The rule: **で = where an action happens; に = where something *is***. 公園**に**います (I *am* in the park — existing there). 公園**で**走ります (I *run* in the park — an action happening there). The verb tells you which to use.

---

### いる vs ある — Existence

Japanese uses two different verbs for "to exist / to be," and the choice depends entirely on what kind of thing exists.

```
[Living thing] が [Place] に います     → there is/are (animate)
[Object/thing] が [Place] に あります   → there is/are (inanimate)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 猫が部屋にいます。| Neko ga heya ni imasu. | There's a cat in the room. |
| 本が机の上にあります。| Hon ga tsukue no ue ni arimasu. | There's a book on the desk. |
| 駅の前に銀行があります。| Eki no mae ni ginkō ga arimasu. | There's a bank in front of the station. |
| 友達が外にいます。| Tomodachi ga soto ni imasu. | My friend is outside. |
| 財布がバッグの中にあります。| Saifu ga baggu no naka ni arimasu. | My wallet is inside the bag. |

> **Note:** いる covers humans, animals, and characters in fiction — anything that can move under its own power. ある covers everything else: objects, plants, buildings, and even scheduled events (会議があります = there's a meeting). Robots are typically treated as objects (ある) unless the context anthropomorphises them.

> **Common mistake:** Using ある for people. 友達が外に**ある** sounds strange at best, rude at worst — objects have ある, not people. This distinction is instinctive for native speakers.

## Chapter 3 — Counting

Japanese doesn't just use numbers — it pairs them with *counters*, which are suffixes that classify what's being counted. The number reading also changes depending on the counter.

### Basic Numbers

| Number | Kanji | Primary reading | Alternate reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 一 | いち | ひと (with some counters) |
| 2 | 二 | に | ふた (with some counters) |
| 3 | 三 | さん | — |
| 4 | 四 | よん | し |
| 5 | 五 | ご | — |
| 6 | 六 | ろく | — |
| 7 | 七 | なな | しち |
| 8 | 八 | はち | — |
| 9 | 九 | きゅう | く |
| 10 | 十 | じゅう | — |
| 100 | 百 | ひゃく | — |
| 1,000 | 千 | せん | — |
| 10,000 | 万 | まん | — |

> **Note:** 4 (し) and 9 (く) are avoided in some contexts because し sounds like 死 (death) and く sounds like 苦 (suffering). Hospital rooms and floors sometimes skip these numbers. This is cultural awareness, not grammar — but you'll notice it.

---

### Common Counters

Every category of object has its own counter suffix. The number reading often changes based on sound compatibility with the counter.

```
[Number] + [Counter]
```

| Counter | Used for | Example | Romaji |
|---|---|---|---|
| 人 (にん/り) | people | 三人 / 一人・二人 | sannin / hitori, futari |
| 個 (こ) | small, general objects | 二個 | niko |
| 枚 (まい) | flat, thin things | 一枚 | ichimai |
| 本 (ほん) | long, cylindrical things | 三本 | sanbon |
| 冊 (さつ) | bound books | 二冊 | nisatsu |
| 匹 (ひき) | small animals | 一匹 | ippiki |
| 台 (だい) | machines, vehicles | 二台 | nidai |
| 杯 (はい) | cups, glasses, bowls | 一杯 | ippai |

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 学生が三人います。| Gakusei ga sannin imasu. | There are 3 students. |
| りんごを二個買いました。| Ringo wo niko kaimashita. | I bought 2 apples. |
| 紙を一枚ください。| Kami wo ichimai kudasai. | Please give me one sheet of paper. |
| コーヒーを一杯飲みました。| Koohii wo ippai nomimashita. | I drank one cup of coffee. |
| 車を二台持っています。| Kuruma wo nidai motte imasu. | I have two cars. |

> **Note:** Number + counter combinations trigger phonetic changes. 一本 (*ippon*), 三本 (*sanbon*), 六本 (*roppon*) — the pattern follows Japanese sound rules (っ and ん appear before certain sounds). These feel random at first but become natural through exposure. Don't try to memorise all variants at once; learn the counter first and pick up the sound changes as you encounter them.

---

### How Many — いくつ / 何～

To ask "how many," use いくつ for a general count, or attach 何 (なん) before the counter when the category is known.

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| りんごがいくつありますか。| Ringo ga ikutsu arimasu ka. | How many apples are there? |
| 何人いますか。| Nannin imasu ka. | How many people are there? |
| 本を何冊読みましたか。| Hon wo nansatsu yomimashita ka. | How many books did you read? |
| これはいくらですか。| Kore wa ikura desu ka. | How much is this? |

> **Note:** いくつ is also used for ages informally (お子さんはいくつですか = How old is your child?), but 何歳 (*nansai*) is the direct "how old" question.

## Chapter 4 — Time and Temporal Reference

### Telling Time

Time is expressed with 時 (*ji*, hour) and 分 (*fun/pun*, minute). The structure is simply: [hour]時[minute]分です.

```
[Hour] 時 [Minute] 分 です
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 今、三時です。| Ima, sanji desu. | It's 3 o'clock. |
| 授業は九時十五分に始まります。| Jugyō wa kuji jūgofun ni hajimarimasu. | Class starts at 9:15. |
| 十二時半に昼ごはんを食べます。| Jūniji han ni hirugohan wo tabemasu. | I eat lunch at 12:30. |
| 午後七時に帰ります。| Gogo shichiji ni kaerimasu. | I'll be home at 7 PM. |

> **Note:** 半 (*han*) means "half" — 三時半 = 3:30. ごぜん (AM) and ごご (PM) come *before* the time: 午前九時 = 9 AM, 午後三時 = 3 PM. Without them, meaning comes from context.

> **Note on sound changes:** Minute readings change based on the number. 一分 (*ippun*), 三分 (*sanpun*), 六分 (*roppun*), 八分 (*happun*), 十分 (*juppun*). These match the same phonetic rules as counters.

---

### Time Particles — に, から, まで

に marks a specific point in time. から and まで mark the start and end of a duration.

```
[Specific time] に + [action]               ← at a specific time
[Start] から [End] まで + [action]           ← from A until B
[Start] から + [action]                      ← from / since A (no end given)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 七時に起きます。| Shichiji ni okimasu. | I wake up at 7. |
| 月曜日に病院へ行きます。| Getsuyōbi ni byōin e ikimasu. | I go to the hospital on Monday. |
| 九時から五時まで働きます。| Kuji kara goji made hatarakimasu. | I work from 9 to 5. |
| 去年から日本語を勉強しています。| Kyonen kara nihongo wo benkyō shite imasu. | I've been studying Japanese since last year. |

> **Common mistake:** に is **not** used with relative time words like 今日, 明日, 毎日, 来週, etc. These words are inherently time-referencing and don't need に. Say 明日行きます — never 明日**に**行きます. Think of に as "at a specific clock/calendar point." If the word is relative (not a specific date or time), drop the に.

---

### Relative Time Words

These are the building blocks of everyday temporal reference. None of them take the particle に.

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 今日 | kyō | today |
| 明日 / 明日 | ashita / myōnichi | tomorrow (spoken / formal) |
| 昨日 | kinō | yesterday |
| 今週 | konshū | this week |
| 来週 | raishū | next week |
| 先週 | senshū | last week |
| 今月 | kongetsu | this month |
| 来月 | raigetsu | next month |
| 先月 | sengetsu | last month |
| 今年 | kotoshi | this year |
| 来年 | rainen | next year |
| 去年 | kyonen | last year |
| 毎日 | mainichi | every day |
| 毎週 | maishū | every week |

## Chapter 5 — Adjectives

Japanese has two grammatically distinct adjective types. They behave differently in predicate position and conjugate by different rules.

### い-Adjectives

い-adjectives end in い and conjugate directly — the い changes to signal tense and polarity. They modify nouns directly (without な) and can stand as predicates on their own.

```
Present:       高い          (is tall / expensive)
Negative:      高くない       (is not tall)
Past:          高かった       (was tall)
Past negative: 高くなかった   (was not tall)
+ です for polite forms: 高いです, 高くないです, 高かったです
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| この本は面白いです。| Kono hon wa omoshiroi desu. | This book is interesting. |
| 今日は寒くないです。| Kyō wa samukunai desu. | It's not cold today. |
| 子どもの頃、背が高かったです。| Kodomo no koro, se ga takakatta desu. | I was tall as a child. |
| そのカフェは安くておいしいです。| Sono kafe wa yasukute oishii desu. | That café is cheap and delicious. |
| 昨日の映画は長くなかったです。| Kinō no eiga wa nagakunakatta desu. | Yesterday's movie wasn't long. |

> **Common mistake:** いい (*good*) is irregular. Its negative is よくない (not よくない is correct — not いくない) and its past is よかった (not いかった). This is the single most common い-adjective error for beginners.

---

### な-Adjectives

な-adjectives behave more like nouns than adjectives. They require な when directly modifying a noun, and conjugate by changing the following です — not the adjective itself.

```
Before a noun:    [な-adj] + な + [noun]   →  静かな部屋
Predicate:        [な-adj] + です
Negative:         [な-adj] + じゃないです / ではないです
Past:             [な-adj] + でした
Past negative:    [な-adj] + じゃなかったです
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| ここは静かです。| Koko wa shizuka desu. | It is quiet here. |
| 彼女は親切な人です。| Kanojo wa shinsetsu na hito desu. | She is a kind person. |
| 昨日は暇でした。| Kinō wa hima deshita. | I was free yesterday. |
| 部屋はきれいじゃないです。| Heya wa kirei ja nai desu. | The room isn't clean. |
| この仕事は大変でした。| Kono shigoto wa taihen deshita. | This job was tough. |

> **Note:** Many learners assume adjectives ending in い are い-adjectives. They're not all. きれい (*kirei*, beautiful), きらい (*kirai*, disliked), ゆうめい (*yūmei*, famous) are all な-adjectives — they end in the *sound* い but grammatically behave like nouns. When in doubt, look it up; the type is always listed in a dictionary.

---

### Chaining Adjectives — ～くて / ～で

When you want to say two things are true of the same subject (cheap *and* delicious, quiet *and* spacious), you connect the adjectives using their て-form.

```
い-adjective: [stem] + くて + [next clause]
  安い  → 安くて
  面白い → 面白くて

な-adjective: [な-adj] + で + [next clause]
  静かな → 静かで
  便利な → 便利で
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 安くておいしいです。| Yasukute oishii desu. | It's cheap and delicious. |
| 静かで広い部屋です。| Shizuka de hiroi heya desu. | It's a quiet and spacious room. |
| この映画は長くてつまらなかったです。| Kono eiga wa nagakute tsumaranakatta desu. | This movie was long and boring. |
| 彼女は親切で頭がいいです。| Kanojo wa shinsetsu de atama ga ii desu. | She is kind and smart. |

> **Note:** The chained form expresses a neutral list ("X and Y"), not necessarily causal connection. If you want to say "it's cheap *so* I'll buy it," that requires から or ので, not ～くて.

## Chapter 6 — Cause and Question Words

### から vs ので — Because

Both から and ので translate as "because," but they carry different nuance and suit different registers.

**から** states a reason directly and subjectively — it asserts "my reason is X." It sounds more assertive, casual, and personal. Use it with friends, in casual writing, or when the reason is a personal feeling.

**ので** presents a reason as objective or soft — it implies "given that X, naturally Y." It sounds more polite, indirect, and considered. Preferred in polite speech, apologies, requests, and workplace communication.

```
[Reason plain form] + から、[Result]     ← subjective, direct, casual
[Reason plain form] + ので、[Result]     ← objective, soft, more polite
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 疲れたから、休みます。| Tsukareta kara, yasumimasu. | I'm resting because I'm tired. |
| 雨が降っているので、家にいます。| Ame ga futte iru node, uchi ni imasu. | I'm staying home because it's raining. |
| 急いでいるから、あとで話しましょう。| Isoide iru kara, ato de hanashimashō. | I'm in a hurry, so let's talk later. |
| 明日試験があるので、勉強しなければなりません。| Ashita shiken ga aru node, benkyō shinakereba narimasen. | I have an exam tomorrow, so I have to study. |

> **Note:** When making a polite request or excuse, ので always sounds more appropriate than から. 電車が遅れたので遅刻しました sounds like a genuine explanation; 電車が遅れたから遅刻しました sounds like a blunt justification. Same facts, different tone.

---

### Question Words

Japanese question words (疑問詞) slot into the sentence like ordinary nouns. The rest of the sentence structure stays the same — no subject/verb inversion like in English.

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 何 (なに / なん) | nani / nan | what |
| 誰 | dare | who |
| どこ | doko | where |
| いつ | itsu | when |
| どう | dō | how |
| なぜ / どうして | naze / dōshite | why |
| どんな | donna | what kind of |
| どれ | dore | which one (of many) |
| どちら / どっち | dochira / docchi | which (of two) / which way |

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| これは何ですか。| Kore wa nan desu ka. | What is this? |
| トイレはどこですか。| Toire wa doko desu ka. | Where is the restroom? |
| なぜ日本語を勉強していますか。| Naze nihongo wo benkyō shite imasu ka. | Why are you studying Japanese? |
| どんな音楽が好きですか。| Donna ongaku ga suki desu ka. | What kind of music do you like? |
| 誰が来ましたか。| Dare ga kimashita ka. | Who came? |

> **Note:** 何 reads as *nani* before most things but *nan* before words starting with a t/d/n sound or before counters. 何時 (*nanji*, what time), 何人 (*nannin*, how many people), 何番 (*nanban*, which number). The switch is phonetic, not grammatical.

## Chapter 7 — Duration and Frequency

### ～間 — Duration

間 (*あいだ / かん*) expresses a span of time — how long something lasted or how long an action continued.

```
[Duration] + 間 (かん/あいだ) + [action]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 三時間勉強しました。| Sanjikan benkyō shimashita. | I studied for 3 hours. |
| 一週間日本にいました。| Isshūkan nihon ni imashita. | I was in Japan for one week. |
| 二年間日本語を学んでいます。| Ninenkan nihongo wo manande imasu. | I have been learning Japanese for 2 years. |
| 十分間待ちました。| Juppunkan machimashita. | I waited for 10 minutes. |

> **Note:** When combined with numbers, 間 usually reads かん (三時間, 一週間). Used alone or with shorter informal phrases, it can read あいだ. In practice, duration expressions almost always appear with かん — you'll rarely need to think about the distinction.

---

### ～回 — Frequency

回 (*かい*) counts the number of times something occurs. It combines with a time frame and a number to express how often.

```
[Time period] に [number] 回 + [action]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 週に三回ジムに行きます。| Shū ni sankai jimu ni ikimasu. | I go to the gym 3 times a week. |
| 一日に二回薬を飲みます。| Ichinichi ni nikai kusuri wo nomimasu. | I take medicine twice a day. |
| 月に一度、家族に電話します。| Tsuki ni ichido, kazoku ni denwa shimasu. | I call my family once a month. |

> **Note:** 一回 (*ikkai*), 二回 (*nikai*), 三回 (*sankai*), 六回 (*rokkai*), 八回 (*hakkai*), 十回 (*jukkai*) — the sound of the following か changes to a geminate for certain numbers. You'll pick these up naturally from listening.

---

### かかる — To Take (Time or Cost)

かかる expresses the time or money that something requires. The amount is marked with the particle に (for time) or に / が (for cost) depending on context.

```
[Time / money] が / に かかります
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 駅まで十分かかります。| Eki made juppun kakarimasu. | It takes 10 minutes to the station. |
| この仕事は三時間かかりました。| Kono shigoto wa sanjikan kakarimashita. | This job took 3 hours. |
| 修理に五千円かかりました。| Shūri ni gosen-en kakarimashita. | The repair cost 5,000 yen. |
| 日本までどのぐらいかかりますか。| Nihon made dono gurai kakarimasu ka. | How long does it take to Japan? |

## Chapter 8 — Desire and Probability

### ～たい — Want to Do

たい attaches to the verb stem and expresses the speaker's personal desire to do something. It conjugates like an い-adjective.

```
[Verb stem] + たいです            (want to do)
[Verb stem] + たくないです        (don't want to do)
[Verb stem] + たかったです        (wanted to do)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 日本に行きたいです。| Nihon ni ikitai desu. | I want to go to Japan. |
| 今日は外出したくないです。| Kyō wa gaishutsu shitakunai desu. | I don't want to go out today. |
| 何が食べたいですか。| Nani ga tabetai desu ka. | What do you want to eat? |
| 子どもの頃、医者になりたかったです。| Kodomo no koro, isha ni naritakatta desu. | When I was a child, I wanted to become a doctor. |

> **Note:** The direct object of ～たい often takes が instead of を, though を is also accepted. 寿司**が**食べたい and 寿司**を**食べたい are both correct; が sounds slightly more natural and emphatic.

> **Register:** ～たい describes *your own* desire. Using it directly about someone else's desire (田中さんは行きたい) can sound presumptuous — it implies you know their inner feelings. For third-person desire, prefer 田中さんは行きたがっている (*seems to want to go*).

---

### ～ほしい — Want Something

ほしい expresses desire for a *thing* (a noun), not an action. It's an い-adjective and conjugates accordingly.

```
[Object] が ほしいです            (want something)
[Object] が ほしくないです        (don't want something)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 新しいカメラがほしいです。| Atarashii kamera ga hoshii desu. | I want a new camera. |
| 今は何もほしくないです。| Ima wa nani mo hoshikunai desu. | I don't want anything right now. |
| 子どもの頃、犬がほしかったです。| Kodomo no koro, inu ga hoshikatta desu. | When I was a child, I wanted a dog. |

> **Note:** ～たい is for actions (verbs); ～ほしい is for objects (nouns). I **want to eat** sushi → すしが食べ**たい**。I **want** sushi → すしが**ほしい**。Same target, different grammar.

---

### ～でしょう / ～かもしれない — Probability

These two forms express degrees of certainty about something that hasn't happened yet or isn't confirmed.

**でしょう** is fairly confident — "probably." It reflects the speaker's informed expectation.  
**かもしれない** is less certain — "might." It leaves room for doubt.

```
[Plain form] + でしょう         ← probably (speaker is fairly confident)
[Plain form] + かもしれません   ← might (speaker is uncertain)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 明日は晴れるでしょう。| Ashita wa hareru deshō. | It will probably be sunny tomorrow. |
| 彼は来ないかもしれません。| Kare wa konai kamo shiremasen. | He might not come. |
| このレストランは高いでしょう。| Kono resutoran wa takai deshō. | This restaurant is probably expensive. |
| 電車が遅れているかもしれません。| Densha ga okurete iru kamo shiremasen. | The train might be running late. |

> **Note:** でしょう in speech often rises in intonation and becomes a soft question tag — そうでしょう？= "That's right, isn't it?" / "Don't you think?" This is a separate use from probabilistic でしょう.

---

## Chapter 9 — て-form and Requests

The て-form is one of the most versatile verb forms in Japanese. It functions as a connector between clauses and is the base for dozens of grammar patterns. Mastering it early pays off throughout the rest of N5 and beyond.

### Forming the て-form

| Group | Ending | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (く) | く → いて | 書く → 書いて | *(exception: 行く → 行って)* |
| 1 (ぐ) | ぐ → いで | 泳ぐ → 泳いで | |
| 1 (す) | す → して | 話す → 話して | |
| 1 (つ, る, う) | → って | 待つ → 待って, 帰る → 帰って | |
| 1 (む, ぬ, ぶ) | → んで | 飲む → 飲んで, 死ぬ → 死んで | |
| 2 | る → て | 食べる → 食べて | |
| 3 | irregular | する → して, 来る → 来て (きて) | |

> **Note:** 行く (to go) is the one Group 1 exception — it becomes 行って, not 行いて. Almost everything else follows the chart.

---

### ～てください — Please Do

The most direct and common way to make a polite request is て-form + ください.

```
[て-form] + ください
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| ここに名前を書いてください。| Koko ni namae wo kaite kudasai. | Please write your name here. |
| もう一度言ってください。| Mō ichido itte kudasai. | Please say it one more time. |
| 静かにしてください。| Shizuka ni shite kudasai. | Please be quiet. |
| 窓を閉めてください。| Mado wo shimete kudasai. | Please close the window. |
| 右に曲がってください。| Migi ni magatte kudasai. | Please turn right. |

> **Note:** ～てください is polite but direct. In very formal or service contexts, ～ていただけますか (*could you please…?*) sounds more deferential. Among friends, you can drop ください entirely and just use the て-form: 書いて = "write it" / "write this for me."

---

### ～てもいいです / ～てはいけません — Permission and Prohibition

Two て-form patterns handle what is and isn't allowed.

```
[て-form] + もいいです          ← it's okay to / you may
[て-form] + もいいですか        ← may I / is it okay to?
[て-form] + はいけません        ← you must not / it's not allowed
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| ここで写真を撮ってもいいですか。| Koko de shashin wo totte mo ii desu ka. | May I take photos here? |
| 窓を開けてもいいですよ。| Mado wo akete mo ii desu yo. | You may open the window. |
| ここでタバコを吸ってはいけません。| Koko de tabako wo sutte wa ikemasen. | You must not smoke here. |
| 試験中にスマホを使ってはいけません。| Shiken chū ni sumaho wo tsukatte wa ikemasen. | You must not use your phone during the exam. |

> **Note:** In casual speech, ～てもいい is often shortened to ～ていい. ～てはいけない is also shortened to ～ちゃいけない (a contraction of ～てはいけない). Both are common in spoken Japanese.

---

### ～てあげる / ～てもらう / ～てくれる — Giving and Receiving Actions

Japanese tracks the *direction* of favors with remarkable precision. Three patterns handle the three possible directions.

**てあげる** — I (or someone) do something *for* someone else (favour flows outward from me).  
**てもらう** — I receive an action *from* someone else (favour flows inward to me).  
**てくれる** — Someone does something for me on their own initiative (not me asking them to).

```
[て-form] + あげます    ← I do for them / they do for (someone else)
[て-form] + もらいます  ← I receive (an action) from them
[て-form] + くれます    ← they do for me (their initiative)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 友達の荷物を持ってあげました。| Tomodachi no nimotsu wo motte agemashita. | I carried my friend's bags (for them). |
| 先生に漢字を教えてもらいました。| Sensei ni kanji wo oshiete moraimashita. | I had the teacher teach me kanji. |
| 母が弁当を作ってくれました。| Haha ga bentō wo tsukutte kuremashita. | My mother made me a bento (for my benefit). |
| 田中さんが駅まで送ってくれました。| Tanaka-san ga eki made okutte kuremashita. | Tanaka kindly drove me to the station. |

> **Note:** The key question to ask yourself: "Who is the beneficiary?" If the benefit goes *to* someone else, use あげる. If the benefit comes *to me*, use もらう or くれる. もらう vs くれる — both mean the favour came to me, but くれる implies they did it of their own accord.

## Chapter 10 — Actions and Temporality

### ～ている — Ongoing Action and State

ている is a broad, frequently used pattern. It has three distinct meanings that context separates.

**1. Action in progress right now** — like English "-ing."  
**2. Resultant state** — the result of a completed action that still holds.  
**3. Habitual action** — something done regularly.

```
[て-form] + います
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 今、本を読んでいます。| Ima, hon wo yonde imasu. | I am reading a book now. | in progress |
| 彼女は結婚しています。| Kanojo wa kekkon shite imasu. | She is married. | resultant state |
| 毎朝、走っています。| Maiasa, hashitte imasu. | I run every morning. | habitual |
| 窓が開いています。| Mado ga aite imasu. | The window is open. | resultant state |
| 田中さんはあそこに座っています。| Tanaka-san wa asoko ni suwatte imasu. | Tanaka is sitting over there. | state |

> **Note:** The resultant-state meaning trips up learners who assume ている only means "currently doing." 結婚しています does not mean "is in the act of getting married right now" — it means "is in a married state." 知っています (*shitte imasu*) means "I know" (not "I am knowing"). 着ています (*kite imasu*) means "is wearing" (the result of having put it on).

---

### ～てから — After Doing

てから expresses a strict sequence: action A is completed, then action B begins. It implies A must finish before B can start.

```
[て-form] + から、[next action]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 手を洗ってから、食べます。| Te wo aratte kara, tabemasu. | I eat after washing my hands. |
| 宿題をしてから、ゲームをします。| Shukudai wo shite kara, gēmu wo shimasu. | I play games after doing homework. |
| 大学を卒業してから、働き始めました。| Daigaku wo sotsugyō shite kara, hataraki hajimemashita. | I started working after graduating from university. |
| シャワーを浴びてから、寝ます。| Shawā wo abite kara, nemasu. | I sleep after taking a shower. |

> **Note:** てから emphasises *completion and sequence* — A must be done, then B happens. It's not the same as 後で (*after*), which is more loosely "sometime after." てから = "once A is done, then B." 後で = "later, after A."

---

### ～前に / ～後で — Before / After

前に (*mae ni*) and 後で (*ato de*) describe time relative to another action. They're more flexible than てから — they don't require strict sequence or completion.

```
[Dictionary form] + 前に、[action]        ← before doing
[て-form / past plain] + 後で、[action]   ← after doing
[Noun] + の前に / の後で
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 寝る前に歯を磨きます。| Neru mae ni ha wo migakimasu. | I brush my teeth before sleeping. |
| 食べた後で、散歩します。| Tabeta ato de, sanpo shimasu. | I take a walk after eating. |
| 授業の前に、単語を復習しました。| Jugyō no mae ni, tango wo fukushū shimashita. | I reviewed vocabulary before class. |
| 映画の後で、カフェに行きましょう。| Eiga no ato de, kafe ni ikimashō. | Let's go to a café after the movie. |

> **Note:** 前に takes the *dictionary form* — the verb is in its plain non-past form because the action hasn't happened yet at that point. 後で takes the *past plain form* — the action has been completed.

---

### ～ながら — While Doing

ながら attaches to the verb stem to describe two simultaneous actions. The ながら clause is always the *secondary* action; the main verb comes last and is what the subject is primarily doing.

```
[Verb stem] + ながら、[main action]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 音楽を聴きながら勉強します。| Ongaku wo kikinagara benkyō shimasu. | I study while listening to music. |
| 歩きながら電話で話しました。| Arukinagara denwa de hanashimashita. | I talked on the phone while walking. |
| テレビを見ながら、夕ごはんを食べます。| Terebi wo minagara, yūgohan wo tabemasu. | I eat dinner while watching TV. |
| コーヒーを飲みながら、新聞を読みました。| Koohii wo nominagara, shinbun wo yomimashita. | I read the newspaper while drinking coffee. |

> **Note:** ながら requires the same subject for both actions. "I study while she watches TV" cannot use ながら. Both actions must be performed by the same person simultaneously.

## Chapter 11 — Comparison and Rankings

### A は B より ～ — A is More ~ Than B

より (*yori*) marks the baseline of comparison. What comes *before* より is what you're being compared against.

```
A は B より [adjective / adverb] です
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 東京は大阪より大きいです。| Tōkyō wa Ōsaka yori ōkii desu. | Tokyo is bigger than Osaka. |
| バスより電車のほうが速いです。| Basu yori densha no hō ga hayai desu. | The train is faster than the bus. |
| 夏より冬のほうが好きです。| Natsu yori fuyu no hō ga suki desu. | I prefer winter to summer. |
| 昨日より今日のほうが寒いです。| Kinō yori kyō no hō ga samui desu. | Today is colder than yesterday. |

> **Note:** Adding のほうが after the item being described adds emphasis — it makes explicit which side is being elevated. Both structures are grammatically correct, but のほうが is more emphatic and common in speech.

---

### どちらが ～ — Which is More ~?

To ask which of two things has a quality more than the other, use どちら or どっち (casual) before が.

```
A と B と どちらが [adjective] ですか
→ A のほうが [adjective] です  (A is more ~)
→ どちらも [adjective] です    (both are equally ~)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 犬と猫とどちらが好きですか。| Inu to neko to dochira ga suki desu ka. | Which do you like more, dogs or cats? |
| 猫のほうが好きです。| Neko no hō ga suki desu. | I like cats more. |
| コーヒーと紅茶とどちらを飲みますか。| Koohii to kōcha to dochira wo nomimasu ka. | Which would you like to drink, coffee or tea? |

---

### いちばん ～ — The Most ~

いちばん means "number one" and works as a superlative marker — it goes directly before the adjective or adverb.

```
[Group] の中で、[item] が いちばん [adjective] です
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| クラスで田中さんが一番背が高いです。| Kurasu de Tanaka-san ga ichiban se ga takai desu. | Tanaka is the tallest in the class. |
| 日本で一番高い山は富士山です。| Nihon de ichiban takai yama wa Fujisan desu. | Mt. Fuji is the tallest mountain in Japan. |
| 何の科目がいちばん好きですか。| Nani no kamoku ga ichiban suki desu ka. | Which subject do you like the most? |
| このメニューの中でいちばん安い料理はどれですか。| Kono menyū no naka de ichiban yasui ryōri wa dore desu ka. | Which dish on this menu is the cheapest? |

---

## Chapter 12 — Advice, Obligation, and Explanation

### ～たほうがいい — Should / Had Better

たほうがいい expresses advice — suggesting that the listener would benefit from a course of action, or warning them off one. The affirmative uses the past plain form; the negative uses the negative plain form.

```
[Past plain] + ほうがいいです            ← you should do
[Negative plain] + ほうがいいです        ← you shouldn't do
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 早く寝たほうがいいです。| Hayaku neta hō ga ii desu. | You should sleep early. |
| タバコを吸わないほうがいいです。| Tabako wo suwanai hō ga ii desu. | You shouldn't smoke. |
| 傘を持っていったほうがいいですよ。| Kasa wo motte itta hō ga ii desu yo. | You should take an umbrella. |
| 無理をしないほうがいいです。| Muri wo shinai hō ga ii desu. | You shouldn't push yourself too hard. |

> **Note:** たほうがいい is advice, not command. It's softer than ～てください and suggests the speaker thinks this is in the listener's best interest. If you want to make it stronger, use ～なければなりません.

---

### ～なければなりません — Must / Have To

This form expresses obligation — something that must be done. It sounds formal and is common in writing, announcements, and polite spoken Japanese.

```
[Negative stem] + なければなりません   (formal, written / polite speech)
[Negative stem] + ないといけません     (casual spoken equivalent)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 明日までにレポートを出さなければなりません。| Ashita made ni repōto wo dasanakereba narimasen. | I must submit the report by tomorrow. |
| 薬を毎日飲まなければなりません。| Kusuri wo mainichi nomanakere ba narimasen. | I have to take medicine every day. |
| もっと練習しないといけません。| Motto renshū shinai to ikemasen. | I need to practice more. |
| 八時までに行かなきゃ。| Hachiji made ni ikanakya. | I gotta go by 8. *(very casual contraction)* |

> **Note:** ～なきゃ / ～なくちゃ are heavily contracted casual forms of these structures. You'll hear them constantly in anime and everyday speech. They're not for formal use.

---

### ～なくてもいいです — Don't Have To

The inverse of obligation — expressing that something is *not* required.

```
[て-form negative] + もいいです
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 今日は来なくてもいいです。| Kyō wa konakute mo ii desu. | You don't have to come today. |
| 全部食べなくてもいいですよ。| Zenbu tabenakute mo ii desu yo. | You don't have to eat everything. |
| 無理しなくてもいいです。| Muri shinakute mo ii desu. | You don't have to push yourself. |
| ネクタイをしなくてもいいです。| Nekutai wo shinakute mo ii desu. | You don't need to wear a tie. |

---

### ～んです — Explanation and Context

～んです (written as のです) adds an explanatory or contextual nuance to a statement. It signals that you are giving or requesting a reason, background, or justification — not just stating a fact.

```
[Plain form] + んです / のです
[い-adj / plain] + んです
[な-adj / noun] + な + んです
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 頭が痛いんです。| Atama ga itai n desu. | (You see,) I have a headache. |
| どうして遅れたんですか。| Dōshite okureta n desu ka. | Why were you late (can you explain)? |
| ちょっと聞いてもいいんですが…| Chotto kiite mo ii n desu ga… | I was wondering if I could ask you something… |
| 実は、昨日転んだんです。| Jitsu wa, kinō koronda n desu. | The thing is, I fell over yesterday. |

> **Note:** ～んです carries the nuance of "there's a reason / explanation behind this." Without it, 頭が痛いです is just a statement of fact. With it, 頭が痛いんです implies "I'm telling you this because it's relevant — that's why I look pale / can't attend / etc." Use it when you want to signal context, not just relay information.

## Chapter 13 — Quotation and Nominalisation

### ～と思います — I Think That

と marks a quote or thought being reported. と思います (*to omoimasu*) expresses the speaker's personal opinion or belief about something.

```
[Plain form] + と思います
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 明日は雨が降ると思います。| Ashita wa ame ga furu to omoimasu. | I think it will rain tomorrow. |
| 彼は来ないと思います。| Kare wa konai to omoimasu. | I think he won't come. |
| この映画は面白いと思います。| Kono eiga wa omoshiroi to omoimasu. | I think this movie is interesting. |
| 少し難しかったと思います。| Sukoshi muzukashikatta to omoimasu. | I think it was a little difficult. |

> **Note:** The clause before と is always in the plain form, not the polite form — 来ます**と**思います is incorrect; it should be 来る**と**思います. The politeness is carried by the final と思います, not by the embedded clause.

---

### ～という — Called / Named / The Thing That

という (*to iu*) connects a word, name, or clause to a following noun. It means "called," "named," or "the thing/person/idea that is ~."

```
[Word / clause] + という + [noun]
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 「わびさび」という概念があります。| "Wabi-sabi" to iu gainen ga arimasu. | There is a concept called "wabi-sabi." |
| 田中という人を知っていますか。| Tanaka to iu hito wo shitte imasu ka. | Do you know someone called Tanaka? |
| 「一期一会」という言葉はどういう意味ですか。| "Ichi-go ichi-e" to iu kotoba wa dō iu imi desu ka. | What does the phrase "ichi-go ichi-e" mean? |
| もう少し待ってほしいというメッセージが来ました。| Mō sukoshi matte hoshii to iu messēji ga kimashita. | A message came saying they wanted me to wait a little longer. |

---

### ～こと — Nominalisation

こと turns a verb phrase into a noun (nominaliser). This lets you use the action itself as a subject, object, or complement in a larger sentence.

```
[Plain form] + こと
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 日本語を話すことが好きです。| Nihongo wo hanasu koto ga suki desu. | I like speaking Japanese. |
| 毎日運動することが大切です。| Mainichi undō suru koto ga taisetsu desu. | Exercising every day is important. |
| 早起きすることが苦手です。| Hayaoki suru koto ga nigate desu. | I'm not good at getting up early. |
| 彼が来ることを知っていましたか。| Kare ga kuru koto wo shitte imashita ka. | Did you know that he was coming? |

> **Note:** こと and の both nominalise verb phrases, but they have different feel. こと is more abstract and general ("the act of X"). の is more perceptual and concrete ("the situation of X actually happening"). For beginner-level patterns like 好き and 大切, こと is the safe choice.

---

### ～たことがある — Have Done Before (Experience)

This pattern expresses whether someone has ever had the experience of doing something. It's about existence of past experience, not a specific remembered event.

```
[Past plain] + ことがあります     (have (ever) done)
[Past plain] + ことがありません   (have never done)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 富士山に登ったことがあります。| Fujisan ni nobotta koto ga arimasu. | I have climbed Mt. Fuji (before). |
| 納豆を食べたことがありません。| Nattō wo tabeta koto ga arimasen. | I have never eaten natto. |
| 日本に行ったことがありますか。| Nihon ni itta koto ga arimasu ka. | Have you ever been to Japan? |
| あのレストランには行ったことがないです。| Ano resutoran ni wa itta koto ga nai desu. | I've never been to that restaurant. |

> **Note:** This pattern always uses the past plain form (行った, 食べた) regardless of when the experience happened — the question is "does this experience exist in your history?" not "when did it happen?"

---

## Chapter 14 — Potential and Ability

### ～ことができる — Can Do

ことができる is the explicit, compositional way to express ability. It works with any verb by attaching to its dictionary form.

```
[Dictionary form] + ことができます         (can do)
[Dictionary form] + ことができません        (cannot do)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 日本語を話すことができます。| Nihongo wo hanasu koto ga dekimasu. | I can speak Japanese. |
| 明日は来ることができません。| Ashita wa kuru koto ga dekimasen. | I cannot come tomorrow. |
| 一人で料理することができますか。| Hitori de ryōri suru koto ga dekimasu ka. | Can you cook on your own? |

---

### Potential Form

A more natural, compact way to express ability. The potential form is built directly into the verb — no extra words needed.

```
Group 1: change the final u-vowel to e-vowel + る
  書く (kaku)  → 書ける (kakeru)
  飲む (nomu)  → 飲める (nomeru)
  待つ (matsu) → 待てる (materu)

Group 2: remove る, add られる
  食べる (taberu) → 食べられる (taberareru)
  見る (miru)     → 見られる (mirareru)

Group 3:
  する → できる
  来る → 来られる (korareru)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 漢字が読めます。| Kanji ga yomemasu. | I can read kanji. |
| 辛い食べ物が食べられません。| Karai tabemono ga taberaremasen. | I can't eat spicy food. |
| 泳げますか。| Oyogemasu ka. | Can you swim? |
| 明日早く起きられますか。| Ashita hayaku okiraremasu ka. | Can you wake up early tomorrow? |
| 一人でできます。| Hitori de dekimasu. | I can do it by myself. |

> **Note:** With the potential form, the direct object often takes が instead of を. 漢字**が**読める and 漢字**を**読める are both accepted, but が sounds more natural in most contexts — as if ability and object are a pair, not actor-and-target.

> **Register:** In casual speech, Group 2 potential forms are often shortened by dropping ら: 食べられる → 食べれる (*tabereru*). This is common but considered non-standard. You'll hear it frequently in natural conversation.

## Chapter 15 — Excess

### ～すぎる — Too Much / Too ~

すぎる attaches to verb stems and adjective stems to express that something is excessive — "too much" or "too ~." It's a versatile intensifier for negative excess.

```
[Verb stem] + すぎる
[い-adj stem (remove い)] + すぎる
[な-adj] + すぎる
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 昨日、食べすぎました。| Kinō, tabesugimashita. | I ate too much yesterday. |
| この部屋は暑すぎます。| Kono heya wa atsusugimasu. | This room is too hot. |
| 彼女は真面目すぎる。| Kanojo wa majimesugiru. | She is too serious. |
| 説明が難しすぎてわかりません。| Setsumei ga muzukashisugite wakarimasen. | The explanation is too difficult; I don't understand. |
| そのかばんは高すぎて、買えません。| Sono kaban wa takasugite, kaemasen. | That bag is too expensive to buy. |

> **Note:** すぎる conjugates as a Group 2 verb: すぎます, すぎました, すぎて. This means you can chain it with other patterns — 食べすぎ**て**気持ちが悪い (I ate too much and feel sick).

---

### ～すぎ as a Noun

The nominalised form ～すぎ (drop the る) turns the expression into a noun — useful for labels and warnings.

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 食べすぎに注意してください。| Tabesugi ni chūi shite kudasai. | Be careful not to overeat. |
| 飲みすぎはよくないです。| Nomisugi wa yokunai desu. | Drinking too much isn't good. |
| 働きすぎで疲れました。| Hatarakisugi de tsukaremashita. | I got tired from overworking. |

## Chapter 16 — Opinion and Planning

### ～つもりです — Intention

つもりです expresses a personal intention or plan — something you have decided to do or not do. The key feature: the decision is *already made* in the speaker's mind.

```
[Dictionary form] + つもりです           (intend to)
[Negative plain] + つもりです            (intend not to)
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 来年、日本に行くつもりです。| Rainen, nihon ni iku tsumori desu. | I intend to go to Japan next year. |
| もうお酒を飲まないつもりです。| Mō osake wo nomanai tsumori desu. | I intend not to drink anymore. |
| 将来、自分でビジネスをするつもりです。| Shōrai, jibun de bijinesu wo suru tsumori desu. | I intend to run my own business in the future. |
| この本は読まないつもりでしたが、面白かったです。| Kono hon wa yomanai tsumori deshita ga, omoshirokatta desu. | I hadn't intended to read this book, but it was interesting. |

> **Note:** つもり reflects *your own* firm intention. Using it for someone else's plans can sound presumptuous. For plans that might change or that are less certain, ～と思っています or ～予定です is more appropriate.

---

### ～予定です — Scheduled To / Planned

予定 (*yotei*) describes a plan or schedule — often external, official, or mutually agreed upon. It's less personal than つもり and suits concrete, time-based plans.

```
[Dictionary form] + 予定です
[Noun] + の + 予定です
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| 来月、出張の予定です。| Raigetsu, shutchō no yotei desu. | I have a business trip planned next month. |
| 三時に会議が始まる予定です。| Sanji ni kaigi ga hajimaru yotei desu. | The meeting is scheduled to start at 3. |
| 来週の月曜日に引っ越す予定です。| Raishū no getsuyōbi ni hikkōsu yotei desu. | I'm planning to move next Monday. |
| 飛行機は六時に到着する予定です。| Hikōki wa rokuj i ni tōchaku suru yotei desu. | The plane is scheduled to arrive at 6. |

> **Note:** つもり vs 予定 — つもり = I personally intend to (internal decision). 予定 = it is planned / scheduled (often implies coordination with others or a fixed arrangement). If you made a reservation, use 予定. If you've decided you'll do something regardless of others, use つもり.

---

### ～と思います — Expressing Opinion

と思います is used not just for probability (as in Chapter 8) but to express opinions and personal judgments. It signals that what follows is your view, not objective fact — important for sounding natural and humble.

```
[Plain form] + と思います
```

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| この映画は面白いと思います。| Kono eiga wa omoshiroi to omoimasu. | I think this movie is interesting. |
| それはよくないと思います。| Sore wa yokunai to omoimasu. | I think that's not a good idea. |
| 彼女のほうが正しいと思います。| Kanojo no hō ga tadashii to omoimasu. | I think she's the one who's right. |
| もう少し考えたほうがいいと思います。| Mō sukoshi kangaeta hō ga ii to omoimasu. | I think you should think it over a bit more. |

> **Note:** Ending an opinion with と思います makes it sound more tentative and polite than stating it flatly. In Japanese communication, expressing a strong opinion without softening can come across as blunt. と思います is one of the most reliable tools for this.

## Chapter 17 — Ongoing State

### ～中 — In the Middle of / Throughout

中 attaches to nouns to express that something is happening *during* or *throughout* a period or space. The same kanji has two readings with different meanings.

**ちゅう** — in the middle of an activity; currently in that state.  
**じゅう** — spanning the whole of something; throughout a time or space.

```
[Noun] + 中 (ちゅう)    ← currently in that activity or state
[Noun] + 中 (じゅう)    ← spanning the whole thing
```

| Japanese | Reading | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| 会議中 | かいぎちゅう | kaigi chū | in a meeting (right now) |
| 勉強中 | べんきょうちゅう | benkyō chū | currently studying |
| 使用中 | しようちゅう | shiyō chū | in use / occupied |
| 工事中 | こうじちゅう | kōji chū | under construction |
| 世界中 | せかいじゅう | sekai jū | throughout the world |
| 一日中 | いちにちじゅう | ichinichi jū | all day long |
| 日本中 | にほんじゅう | nihon jū | all over Japan |
| 年中 | ねんじゅう | nenjū | all year round |

| Japanese | Romaji | English |
|---|---|---|
| すみません、今、電話中です。| Sumimasen, ima, denwa chū desu. | Sorry, I'm on the phone right now. |
| 彼女は一日中働いています。| Kanojo wa ichinichi jū hataraite imasu. | She works all day long. |
| 使用中のため入れません。| Shiyō chū no tame hairemasen. | Cannot enter — currently in use. |
| 世界中の人が知っています。| Sekai jū no hito ga shitte imasu. | People all over the world know about it. |
| 日本中を旅行しました。| Nihon jū wo ryokō shimashita. | I travelled all over Japan. |

> **Note:** The kanji 中 is the same in both cases — only the reading and meaning change. ちゅう = during / in the middle of (the activity is in progress). じゅう = spanning the whole thing (no part is excluded). 勉強中 (studying right now) vs 一日中勉強した (studied all day without stopping). When you see 中 attached to a noun, ask: is it "currently doing X" or "throughout all of X"?
